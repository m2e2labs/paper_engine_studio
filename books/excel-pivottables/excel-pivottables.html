Why PivotTables Exist

A PivotTable answers many questions about one list.

Let's say your sales list is finished. Someone asks for the total by region, so you write a SUMIF. Then they want it by salesperson, then by product. Every new question is another formula to write, check and keep right.

A PivotTable is an Excel tool that calculates, summarises and analyses a list, so you can see comparisons, patterns and trends. The first book called it a summary table. It's the same thing, and from here on this book uses its proper name.

One list can answer many questions.

Data a PivotTable Can Read

A PivotTable needs a tidy list with no gaps.

Let's say you're about to build your first PivotTable. Blank rows are exactly what Microsoft says to avoid.

The shape to aim for is a tidy list, like the Sales sheet: one row per sale, one row of headings across the top, and no blank rows or columns inside it. That's a list a PivotTable can read.

Tidy list in, answers out.

Tidying a List Before You Pivot

Run a short checklist before you build.

Let's say a colleague sends you their sales list and you want to pivot it. You can't tell by looking whether it's in good shape.

Microsoft's advice comes down to a short checklist. Every column has a heading, and no two headings are the same. There's one row of headings, not two. No cells are merged. And no column mixes dates and text.

Tidy the list first, then build.

DO THIS NOW

On the Sales sheet, check the top row has a different heading on every column, then look for merged cells.

Inserting Your First PivotTable

Select your list, then choose Insert and PivotTable.

Let's say your list is tidy and you're ready to build. It's natural to worry that building a PivotTable will change your list.

Select the cells you want to build from, then choose Insert and PivotTable.

A PivotTable works from a snapshot of your data, called the cache. Building one doesn't change your list.

Your list stays as it is.

DO THIS NOW

Click the first cell on the Sales sheet, choose Insert, then PivotTable, and click OK.

The Field List and Its Four Boxes

Drag headings into four boxes to build one.

Let's say your new PivotTable has a long list of headings beside it. What are you meant to do with them?

That list is the Field List, and each heading is a field. Drag fields into its four boxes to build the PivotTable, or tick one and Excel picks a box: text goes to Rows, numbers to Values.

If the Field List isn't showing, click inside the PivotTable, then choose Analyze and Field List.

Where you drop a heading decides what it does.

DO THIS NOW

Tick Region in the Field List. Then drag Amount into the Values box.

Reading Row Labels and Grand Totals

Know what each part of a finished PivotTable is.

Let's say your PivotTable shows a block of numbers. A number you can't place is a number you can't trust.

Row labels run down the left, column labels run across the top, and the numbers in the middle are your values.

Excel also adds subtotals and a Grand Total by itself, and you can show or hide them.

Labels tell you what, values tell you how much.

DO THIS NOW

In your Region PivotTable, find the Grand Total. On the Sales sheet, type =SUM(G:G) in an empty cell beside the list and check the two match.

Excel's Recommended PivotTables

Excel can suggest a layout for you to start from.

Let's say you're not sure which headings to drag where.

Excel has a feature called Recommended PivotTables. It works out a layout for your data, which you can then rearrange.

Two things to know. Excel sends your data to an AI service for this, and opting out of connected experiences switches it off. On the web it needs a Microsoft 365 subscription too.

Treat a suggestion as a start, not the answer.

DO THIS NOW

Decide now whether the data in your list is fine to send to an AI service. If it isn't, build your PivotTable by hand, as the earlier lessons showed.

Practice: Your First PivotTable

Build Amount by Region, then check the total.

Let's say you want the total sales for each region, and you want to be sure it's right.

Choose Insert, then PivotTable, and pick New Worksheet. Tick Region and Amount: text goes to Rows, and numbers go to Values, where Excel adds them up by default.

The Grand Total should match a SUM of the Amount column.

A PivotTable you've checked is one you can trust.

DO THIS NOW

Build Amount by Region on a new sheet. Then type =SUM(G:G) in an empty cell on the Sales sheet and compare.
